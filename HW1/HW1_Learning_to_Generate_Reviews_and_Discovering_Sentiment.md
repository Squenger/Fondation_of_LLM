**Aimine Meddeb**

This article is very interesting due to its form, the way the researchers wrote the paper, and the pioneering nature of the method depicted.

* First, they don't present supervised learning as the royal road to training a model (which is the feeling I have when I read other papers); on the contrary, they try to show that unsupervised learning can develop complex representations without any direction given by a human. We always hear about big LLMs trained by companies like OpenAI or Anthropic, which are trained on the largest datasets possible with the most powerful GPUs. However, I am more drawn to researchers or companies that work on new and smarter ways to train a model, because I think it is the only way this industry can remain coherent with today's ecological challenges.
* Also, the word that grabbed my attention in the title was "sentiment," because it is really interesting to see how a model (especially an LLM) simulates things that we cannot clearly describe ourselves. Indeed, I really appreciate when researchers dive into a complex model and disassemble it to understand how it works. It is like reverse-engineering the model, which is, in my opinion, too often considered an incomprehensible black box.
* I feel as though the core of the article (the discovery of a unique weight that represents a positive or negative aspect of a sentence) was not the original objective of the research. It is as if they discovered this phenomenon in the middle of their work and realized it was more interesting than what they had initially set out to do. In this article, I really sense this in the flow of the writing, and it makes the article more pleasant to read.
* Today, training a large model that is afterwards fine-tuned for a specific task is a well-known method. However, if we look at the publication date of this paper (April 2017), we realize that it is a foundational paper for transfer learning in NLP, demonstrating the effectiveness of generative modeling well before the appearance of Transformers (June 2017).

The result that surprised me the most in this article is clearly the extreme sparsity of the final classifier. Theoretically, one could have predicted that the polarity of a sentence can be encoded in a 1-dimensional vector: the higher the value, the more positive the sense is. This could be a model that a human would make because it is the simplest option (more dimensions to encode this are not necessary). However, this simple way of encoding sentiment was also found by a model, and this result really caught my attention. Even though sparsity is a consequence of L1 regularization, I was not expecting the model to reach such a precise modeling. Indeed, in my (short) experience of training models, my natural feeling toward neural networks is that they create good results by "brute force": they create such a complex (often too complex) representation space that they are able to recreate complex structures. But in this case, the researchers showed the contrary. Furthermore, the result they present is so clear and well controlled that it is not only an observation: they are able to force the model to generate positive or negative reviews (by modifying the value of the sentiment weight) and see the generative probability distribution adapt instantaneously to produce positive or negative text.
This shows that this neuron causally governs the generation process.

Even if this article was, in the end, very fascinating, I have to admit that I was a little disappointed when I realized that what they call "sentiment" in the title was only the negative/positive polarity of a review. In my mind, I imagined a more complex distribution with human sentiments as we imagine them—for example, anger, happiness, sadness, etc. However, this article can be a baseline for a more complex sentiment representation. For instance, one could imagine exactly the experiment they did, but with a more complex dataset that represents a larger diversity of feelings. After that, one could fine-tune a classifier with labeled texts associated with a set of feelings (anger, sadness, happiness, ...) and then it could be very interesting to see whether we can recover a subset of weights (ideally with a number of dimensions equal to the number of feelings the dataset was annotated with) and try to recover the same results as in the paper. I think this could be a very interesting extension of this paper.
